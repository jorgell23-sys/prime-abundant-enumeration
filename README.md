## What was found

The prime-abundant numbers — the `n` with `rad(n) | σ(n)`, OEIS A175200 — can be
listed **without sieving**, because the defining condition forces every prime of
`n` to appear when factoring a value of `σ`; this puts the list at `10^13`
instead of `10^7`, giving 85,695 terms where 10,000 were published.

<!-- hallazgo:enunciado -->
## The statement

Write `S(f) = { n : rad(n) | f(n) }` for `f` multiplicative and **local**
(`p ∤ f(p^a)`), and let `D_f(n)` be the digraph on the primes of `n` with an
arrow `q → p` when `p | f(q^{v_q(n)})`.

- `n ∈ S(f)` **iff every vertex of `D_f(n)` has an incoming arrow**.
- Hence **no prime of `n` is free**: each one divides `f(q^a)` for another prime
  power `q^a` of `n`. And a finite digraph with all in-degrees `≥ 1` contains a
  cycle from which every vertex is reachable.
- Therefore `S(f)(X)` is generated by choosing directed cycles and adding
  descendants, pruning by `product ≤ X`, and **if `q^a | n ≤ X` then every other
  prime of `n` is `≤ X/q^a`** — which is what keeps the factoring cheap.

Counts, with the row at `10^9` being the one already in print:

| `x` | prime-abundant | prime-perfect |
|---:|---:|---:|
| `10^9` | **5,328** | **198** |
| `10^10` | 11,051 | 333 |

## The smallest case, done by hand

`n = 6 = 2·3`. Then `rad(6) = 6`, and `σ(6) = 1+2+3+6 = 12`. Since `12 = 2·6`,
`rad(6) | σ(6)` and `6` is prime-abundant.

Read as a digraph: `σ(2) = 3`, so `2 → 3`; and `σ(3) = 4 = 2²`, so `3 → 2`. Both
vertices have an incoming arrow — that is the whole criterion, and it is a cycle
of length 2.

Now the point of the method. From `6` the search does **not** try `7, 8, 9, …`.
It asks which primes divide `σ(2³) = 1+2+4+8 = 15 = 3·5`: the prime `5` appears,
and `5` does not divide `24 = 2³·3` (which is prime-abundant, since
`σ(24) = 60` and `rad(24) = 6`). So `24·5^c ∈ S(σ)` for every `c ≥ 1` — in
particular `120`, the eighth term of A175200, where `rad(120) = 30` and
`σ(120) = 360 = 12·30`. Next comes `600`, then `3000`. `verify.py` checks that
consequence for `c = 1, 2, 3`.

The same reasoning is what puts the smallest element of `S(Φ₆)` at `100009`
instead of `39`: same arrows, higher exponents.

## Why it is proved

`p` is prime, so `p` divides a product iff it divides a factor; applying that to
`rad(n) | f(n) = ∏ f(q^{v_q(n)})` gives the digraph criterion exactly. Reading
it backwards, every prime of `n` receives an arrow, so every prime of `n` is a
prime divisor of some `f(q^a)` with `q^a || n` — nothing is free. Walking
backwards along arrows from any vertex cannot go on forever without repeating,
so it closes a cycle; hence every vertex lies on or after a cycle. Finally,
adding a vertex that is already covered cannot uncover anyone, because the
exponents of the vertices present do not change — so every partial construction
is itself an element of `S(f)`, and the enumeration is complete.

prime-abundant numbers."*

## What it does not say

- **It does not claim the enumeration idea is new.** It follows directly from
  the digraph characterisation, which is not new — Pollack and Pomerance already
  use the trees `T(p)` that are a restriction of it. No published statement of
  this enumeration was found, but not finding one is not evidence. See
  `PRIOR_ART.md`. **What is new is the data**: the published b-file stops at
  7,271,402,112 and the published counts stop at `10^9`.
- **It does not give an asymptotic.** The effective exponent
  `α = d log|S|/d log x` falls from 0.45 to 0.288 over the measured range and
  does not settle; that is compatible with the known bounds and proves nothing
  about the limit.
- **It does not predict `|S(f)(x)|` from `f`.** Six candidate invariants were
  ruled out, each against a criterion fixed in advance. The best predictor found
  is off by a factor of 2.3 out of sample. That is a negative result and it is
  reported as one.
- **Exhaustive cross-checking reaches `10^10`, not `10^13`.** Beyond `10^10`
  each term is verified individually — which rules out false positives, not
  false negatives.
